Synopsis

The July 23 session opened with two public hearings on proposed Local Law No. 4 of 2026, a charter amendment that would replace the city's emergency declaration standard with language aligned with New York State Executive Law Article 2-B. All five speakers supported the legislation. Residents documented a pattern of emergency declarations the mayor used to bypass procurement rules and council oversight. Francis Sweet catalogued ten declarations the mayor had issued -- some announced only on Facebook with no other notice -- and reported that a FOIL request turned up documentation for only eight. "So two of these were just completely made up," he said. The public hearings follow the council's 7-0 veto override of Mayor Mantello's attempt to block them on July 9.

The Science & Technology Committee concluded its third and final BESS moratorium check-in and identified two remaining requirements before the draft ordinance is ready: an emergency response financial assurance bond from any BESS operator, and stronger decommissioning provisions. Committee Chair Struber also raised an immediate problem with the city's ADA compliance: complaint forms on troyny.gov have not been updated since 2017 and list a contact whose email may no longer be active. Any resident who filed an ADA grievance under the current administration may have received no response.

The Finance Committee approved a preliminary authorization for the Monument Square project -- 51 apartments and roughly 40,000 square feet of office space at an estimated $50 million -- subject to a required public hearing before any final transfer of city-owned parcels. Struber led close questioning on the GIVE grant's Clear data aggregation tool, pressing the police chief on audit frequency. The committee also approved a $125,000 DASNY grant for downtown camera upgrades, with Struber moving to amend the resolution to name Eclipse as the vendor. A $15.5 million bond authorization enables the city to apply for state water system reconstruction funding. The session also honored C.R.A.B. Garrison 1954, which has served Troy's veterans for more than 70 years, and designated Rensselaer Street west of River Street as Super 400 Way.

At the Special Meeting, residents pressed the council on surveillance technology oversight -- questioning whether the GIVE grant's Clear tool was being rubber-stamped without the same democratic process applied to Flock -- and asked when the Flock 60-day and 120-day audit results would be made public.

Public Hearings: Emergency Powers Reform (Local Law No. 4)

Public Hearing #1 called to order at 5:17 p.m. All seven members present. Public Hearing #2 called to order at 5:30 p.m. Public Hearing clerk's minutes not yet published; proceedings from transcript.

Five speakers addressed the council on Local Law No. 4, a charter amendment that would replace the city's emergency declaration standard with language aligned with New York State Executive Law Article 2-B. Every speaker supported it.

Frankie, a Troy resident, called the mayor's emergency declarations a pattern of bypassing procurement rules and council oversight rather than responding to genuine crises. "We're wasting so much precious time on the mayor's fake emergency when thousands of Troy residents are facing actual emergencies," she said, citing housing, healthcare, and childcare costs.

Francis Sweet of District 6 catalogued ten declarations the mayor had issued -- some announced only on Facebook with no other notice. A FOIL request turned up documentation for only eight. "So two of these were just completely made up," he said. His point: systems with clear rules hold up until someone decides not to follow them. Troy now needs to close the gaps.

Danielle Larose of District 18 joined online and said nothing in the administration's recent conduct gave her confidence the emergency power would not be abused again.

Bri Harrington of District 4 (10th Street) cited the $80,000 spent under the Flock camera emergency declaration. "We definitely could have used that $80,000 for other things." Noel Damash of District 2 (Oakwood Avenue) also spoke in support.

Science & Technology Committee: City Website ADA Compliance

Committee called to order at 5:45 p.m. Council Members Struber, DiLorenzo, Favreau, Campbell-Cohen, and Council President Steele present. Science & Technology clerk's minutes not yet published; proceedings from transcript.

CivicPlus, the city's current website host, can add two modules to bring troyny.gov into compliance with WCAG 2.1 standards: an audio management implementation and a document accessibility platform (DocAccess). One-time cost: $22,910.80. Annual fee: $19,410.80, with a 5% increase in year two. Both are new expenses with no current budget line.

The original compliance deadline was April 2026. A federal extension arrived one day before it came due. The new target is August.

Committee Chair Struber raised a more immediate problem: ADA complaint forms on troyny.gov have not been updated since 2017 and list a contact whose email may no longer be active. Any resident who filed an ADA grievance since the current administration took office may have received no response. Struber asked that those forms be corrected first.

The committee asked whether city departments could be trained to produce ADA-compliant PDFs before submitting content, reducing the burden on the two-person BIS team. The administration said that was under consideration. The BIS team offered to arrange a CivicPlus demo so council members can evaluate the modules before a budget decision is made.

Science & Technology Committee: BESS Moratorium -- Third and Final Check-in

This was the third and final check-in under the BESS moratorium. Fire Chief DeWolf and Building Inspector Carlo Arzoumanian attended. Council Member McLaren also joined a recent fire department site visit.

Since the last meeting, Carlo connected with the Town of Warwick, which had a BESS fire in which one or two containers burned for four days. A school 200 yards from the site was evacuated. The suspected cause was water infiltration. The chief's concern is not the department's ability to manage an incident -- Troy has the training -- but the financial exposure of a four-to-five-day round-the-clock response.

Two additions remain before the departments are satisfied with the draft ordinance:

  • Emergency response financial assurance: an upfront bond, escrow, or surety from any BESS operator to cover city costs -- police, fire, utilities, engineering -- if an emergency occurs. Recovering costs after the fact is very difficult.
  • Stronger decommissioning language: the current eight-page draft addresses decommissioning, but the departments want the provisions tightened.

On setbacks: Carlo said a 500-foot requirement would functionally ban BESS in Troy because no parcel could likely comply without a zoning variance. Setbacks should be proportionate to the facility's size and measured from the battery units themselves, not the fence line. A facility can sit 30 to 40 yards inside a property boundary, so the actual distance from neighbors may be much smaller than the stated setback. Even one or two containers burning produces the same air contaminants as a full-facility fire.

State codes training for fire and building officials is set for approximately October 23. The goal: a sit-down meeting with the administration in coming weeks, draft legislation at the August Finance Committee, and a final vote in September.

Finance Committee: C.R.A.B. Garrison Way, Res. 74

Finance Committee called to order at 6:23 p.m. All seven members present. In attendance: Mayor Carmella Mantello, Deputy Mayor Seamus Donnelly, Commissioner of General Services Joe Mazzariello, Corporation Counsel Richard Morrissey, Comptroller Michael McNeff, Communications Director Alex Horton, developer Christian Luizzi, Chief of Police Daniel DeWolf, DA Mary Pat Donnelly, Officer David Dean, Andrew Golden, and Chris Arzoumanian. Approximately 15 members of the public attended.

The council voted on Resolution 74 first so members of Crab Garrison 1954 could be present for the ceremony without waiting through the rest of the agenda.

C.R.A.B. stands for Chorelli, Ray, Ares, and Bevino -- four servicemen from South Troy killed in defense of the United States. Three died in World War II; one in the Korean War. The organization was founded in 1954 by their families and neighbors to ensure those four men would not be forgotten. The Crab Garrison building on Liberty Street was purchased from the city for $1, with a commitment to serve veterans and the community. The organization now has more than 400 members.

Mayor Mantello said she grew up remembering the Crab Club and other neighborhood organizations as thriving institutions. The clubs declined as the next generation stopped joining. Now they're coming back. Council President Steele told the crowd she received a Crab Club award when she graduated from Troy High in 1971.

Commander Josh spoke for the organization. "A street name becomes part of everyday life. People will travel this road. They'll look up. Perhaps they'll pause. They'll say: 'What is Crab Garrison 1954?'" He called that question the door into the organization's story.

The designation covers the portion of Liberty Street in front of the building. A formal dedication ceremony will be scheduled.

Finance Committee: Public Forum

Greg Lion of Second Street asked the council to postpone Resolutions 77 and 78, both of which expand police surveillance technology. He said the council spent six months scrutinizing Flock and should not approve more surveillance tools without first passing legislation governing their use.

Frankie returned from the public hearings to raise concerns about Resolution 76 and the Troy Local Development Corporation. The deputy mayor is the TLDC's executive director, and its board includes political allies of the mayor. With no independent distance between the two entities, she said she could not see how the TLDC makes independent decisions on city real estate. She also said the public was not being told the full extent of any tax breaks or subsidies attached to these deals.

Francis Sweet of District 6 asked about the guardrails on Clear, the AI-driven social media monitoring tool funded by the GIVE grant. The grant documentation says monitoring covers not just suspects but their affiliates, family, and friends -- people who have not committed crimes. He asked where it ends and who counts as an affiliate. He also asked why Resolution 78 named no vendor.

Finance Committee: Monument Square -- TLDC, Res. 76 & 79

Christian Luizzi of Louisiana Properties presented the Monument Square project: roughly 40,000 square feet of office space and 51 apartments, priced above market rate. Total cost: $50 million. The project would be built to the same standard as Louisiana Properties' development across the river where the Starbucks building stands. Parking will exceed the initial estimate of 107 spaces.

The riverfront behind the building would be redesigned as public park space, donated to the city and maintained by the developer. New York State grant funding is essential to the project's finances. Council Member Favreau, a member of the ARPA steering committee, noted that $2 million in ARPA funds are also going toward it and asked whether spending that money by year-end was a concern. Luizzi said it was not.

At a previous public meeting, residents asked for a pharmacy, a public bathroom, a Trader Joe's, and a playground. Luizzi said the team recorded every suggestion and will respond to each as engineering and architecture work advances. Favreau, a former downtown resident, pushed hard for a playground: "downtown really needs one."

Resolution 76 is a preliminary approval only. A public hearing is required before any final sale of city-owned parcels to the TLDC can occur. Resolution 79 is the associated SEQRA Type II determination.

Finance Committee: GIVE Grant -- Res. 77

Chief DeWolf briefed the committee on the GIVE (Group Intervention Violence Enforcement) grant, now in its 13th year. It funds four positions: a field intelligence officer, an anti-violence coordinator, a non-fatal shooting sergeant, and a crime analyst -- salary and fringe benefits for all four. The total GIVE program runs about $1.2 million; this resolution covers the city's share.

Struber led close questioning on Clear (Consolidated Lead Evaluation and Reporting), a data aggregation tool in its second year with the department. Clear pulls public records -- addresses, phone numbers, prior associations -- from multiple sources into one system. Five officers have access. Troy does not enter data into Clear; it queries only. DeWolf said the primary uses are fraud investigations and fugitive apprehension. Struber asked who audits access and how often. DeWolf said the FIO administers the system and auditing mechanisms exist, but he did not know the frequency and offered to find out.

Anti-violence coordinator Carrie Hoover, a retired Troy officer, runs park walk-and-talks, community barbecues at the ICC, the Market, and Geneva-Pompy Park, and a quarterly parole re-entry call-in pairing recently released individuals with service providers. She also writes the grant and handles all reporting. DCJS publishes sanitized outcome data on its website.

Finance Committee: Downtown Camera Upgrade -- Res. 78

The SAM (State and Municipalities) grant from DASNY will fund a $125,000 upgrade to the city's fixed downtown CCTV cameras. The existing Eclipse cameras, installed roughly five years ago, have degraded to the point where footage is too grainy to hold up in prosecutions -- a problem the DA's office flagged.

The upgrade covers 15 locations at downtown intersections. New cameras have pan, tilt, and zoom. Data is stored on city servers with local backups. No cloud storage, no facial recognition, no AI.

Struber moved an amendment naming Eclipse -- a local vendor on state contract, based off New Corner Road -- in the resolution text. Corporation Counsel Morrissey added the language to the first "whereas" clause and the operative paragraph. The amendment passed with the resolution. The grant has not been finally awarded; DASNY requires council authorization before making its final decision.

Finance Committee: Water System Bond Authorization -- Res. 80

The bond resolution authorizes up to $15.5 million for reconstruction of the city's water supply and distribution system. The authorization is a prerequisite for a state grant -- the WE2 program -- not a commitment to issue bonds at that level. The city's existing backup generator at the water plant dates to 1999 and would be replaced as part of the project. The system runs at 4,160 volts, too high for solar or battery backup.

Finance Committee: Super 400 Way -- Res. 81

The council designated the portion of Rensselaer Street west of River Street as Super 400 Way, honoring the 30-year career of the Troy band that has been active since 1996.

Struber said she researched the band after seeing the designation fall in her district and realized she had watched them accept an award at the Eddies in 2023. Favreau has followed Super 400 for 10 to 15 years, printed their merchandise, and taken piano lessons at their downtown studio. "A lot of the things they're putting on is free," she said, and encouraged residents to check out the Troy Music Academy. Council Member Spain-McLaren noted that guitarist Kenny's late mother, Bonnie, was her mother-in-law's first cousin. Troy is a small town.

Special Meeting: Public Forum

Special Meeting called to order at 7:39 p.m. All seven members present. Same administration attendance as Finance.

Francis Sweet raised a pointed discrepancy: the GIVE grant documentation focuses on violent crime, but Chief DeWolf had emphasized fraud in his Finance presentation. He read a comment submitted by another resident: "If you spent six months establishing that Flock data sharing needed democratic oversight, why are you rubber-stamping a grant that funds a more invasive tool with zero local use policy attached?"

Greg Lion asked when results from the Flock 60-day and 120-day audit windows -- which he said had passed -- would be made public.
