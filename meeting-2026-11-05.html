Crow Contract Rejected & E-Moto Resolution

November 5, 2026 | Troy City Council Regular Meeting

← Back ▶ Watch on YouTube Regular Meeting Agenda Regular Meeting Minutes

Synopsis

The council voted 0-7 against the administration's contract with the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) to haze the downtown crow roost, after residents wrote in overwhelmingly against it. The council also backed a state bill to regulate the sale of e-motos, accepted the outside audit of the city's 2025 finances, honored Gloria Steinem, and approved a parcel auction after striking one property from the list. Every vote was unanimous.

Council President Sue Steele said she asked the administration what prompted the crow request and received no answer. In the open forum, a resident disputed a $175 bulk trash bill that the resident said charges a fee found nowhere in the city code, and tobacco-free advocates asked the council to put Troy's "no vaping" park signs into law.

Resolution 99: Crow Hazing Contract Fails 0-7

Regular Meeting, Council President Steele presiding. Called to order at 7:00 p.m. Present: Council Members Phil DiLorenzo, Shikole Struber, Kathleen Spain-McLaren, Noreen McKee, Jona Favreau, and Greg Campbell-Cohen, and Council President Steele. In attendance: Mayor Carmella Mantello, Deputy Mayor Seamus Donnelly, Acting Corporation Counsel Richard Morrissey, and Communications Director Alex Horton. Adjourned at 7:53 p.m.

The council unanimously rejected the crow hazing contract. The program would have used loud noise devices to break up the crow roost downtown this winter. The USDA official who would run it answered the council's questions at the October 29 session. Every member who spoke cited resident opposition, the lack of a public health threat, and the city's tight budget.

Council Member Struber said she asked three questions: do residents want it, does it solve a problem, and what else could the city do? Of 45 written comments the council had received by the time she left home, 36 opposed hazing, and three of the four she could confirm came from District 2 were against it. The USDA representative and other studies found no real public health risk from the droppings, she said, which leaves inconvenience. "It's not our job, nor our place, to try to control wild animals in their natural habitats," she said. She suggested power washing sidewalks more often, and pointed to Austin, Texas, which turned its bat colony into an annual festival that helps pay for cleanup.

Council Member McKee said she read every email and researched the dispersal methods, and the benefits don't outweigh the costs. With the mayor's recent hiring freeze over revenues that may come in light, she said, the money would be better spent on things like filling potholes.

Council Member DiLorenzo weighed it against the weeks of budget hearings. The city hasn't hazed the crows since 2022, he said. Public Utilities is raising water and sewer rates, a large transfer to the General Fund appears to be coming, and departments are cutting back, down to a smaller gasoline budget for the city garage. With methods that are "temporary at best" against birds that live here, he said, spending thousands now isn't the best option.

Council Member Favreau said only four District 5 residents wrote in, likely because the crows roost downtown and not up the hill. She once lived on Fifth Avenue under the roost, had her car covered in droppings, and learned where to park. The hazing ran while she lived there, and she didn't see it work. She'd rather put the money toward pressure washing and picking up dog waste downtown. The USDA work wouldn't have started until December, she noted, so the council could revisit it if the crows return in force.

Council Member Campbell-Cohen said several dozen District 6 residents contacted him, many in the Osgood neighborhood where the sound carries past the canal, and every one opposed the hazing. That alone would decide his vote, he said. The "screamer" devices run at 100 decibels, which he said is hard on people and pets with sensory sensitivities. "Quality of life also includes the things that you are fond of," he said, "like experiencing birds."

Steele said she was torn. She likes the crows, and the council heard overwhelming support for them. She also wanted to set the record straight: the mayor seems to think opposition is about the mayor, she said, but "this issue is not about the mayor, it's about crows." She asked the administration what prompted the request and received nothing back. Only two residents, both downtown, urged hazing. Her remaining worry was Watervliet: she had spoken with that city's mayor and wondered whether a neighbor's hazing could push more crows into Troy. She voted no.

Resolution 100: Backing State Rules on E-Moto Sales

The council voted 7-0 to support state Senate bill S10700, which would regulate the sale of e-motos and electric off-highway motorcycles in New York. Favreau co-sponsored it with Steele, and Campbell-Cohen joined as a sponsor from the floor.

Cities have a hard time enforcing e-moto rules, Campbell-Cohen said, because the bikes can be bought in one town and ridden in the next, so regulation works best at the state level. DiLorenzo said e-bikes have their place for commuting, but he has heard of District 1 residents seriously injured by e-motos recently and wants the council to keep talking about it.

The 2025 Audit, Gloria Steinem, and a Parcel Auction

Resolution 97 accepts the 2025 financial statement audit and the single audits of federal and state Department of Transportation spending, all performed by the Bonadio Group. It passed 7-0 without discussion. The auditors walked the council through their findings at the October 22 session.

Resolution 98 honors the life and legacy of Gloria Steinem. Its sponsor, McKee, said Steinem spent more than 60 years working for equal rights. "I can honestly say I would not be sitting here if it had not been for the women's movement," McKee said. Steele agreed. It passed 7-0.

Ordinance 48 authorizes the sale of city-owned parcels at auction. Favreau moved to strike 2215 Old Sixth Avenue from the list, citing City Code Chapter 83-4, section B-2, and Campbell-Cohen seconded. The amendment passed 7-0, and the amended ordinance passed 7-0. Ordinance 47, a transfer of funds within the 2026 General Fund, and Resolution 101, appointing a commissioner of deeds, passed 7-0 without discussion.

Storm Disaster Loans: Apply by November 23

A planned presentation from the U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA) fell through when Patrick Gant, its lead public affairs specialist, couldn't attend, so Steele read his message. Troy homeowners, renters, businesses, and nonprofits hit by the July 28 and 29 storms and flooding may qualify for SBA disaster loans to repair or replace damaged property. The deadline for physical damage loans is November 23, 2026. Small businesses and nonprofits can also apply for an economic injury loan to cover storm-related operating costs, even without property damage, until June 24, 2027. Applications are at sba.gov/disaster.

Public Forum: Agenda Items

Three residents spoke, all against the crow hazing contract.

Frances Sweet of District 6 pointed to the USDA representative's own presentation at the previous meeting, where he said the hazing might not work and crews often have to come back the same year. That makes it a waste of $13,000 on a problem the city doesn't know it can solve, Sweet said. Garbage draws crows, so keeping the streets clean would do more.

Robin, a downtown resident at a council meeting for the first time, said they walk Broadway constantly and haven't seen a crow problem in two years of living there. Robin suggested neighbors bothered by the birds hang reflective objects in trees, and asked the council to put the money toward cleanup. "When we said defog Troy," Robin said, "we meant the cameras, not the birds."

Angela Beeler of Jacob Street said hazing doesn't make crows leave. It pushes them to the next municipality, which pushes them back. Development like Starbuck Island has taken away places the birds once roosted, Beeler said. Beeler asked whether anyone had studied alternatives since Troy began working with the USDA in the late 1990s, and noted that this winter brings the fifth annual crow procession, a community walk celebrating the roost.

Public Forum: Any Topic

Dana Santiago of Eighth Avenue disputed a $175 bulk trash bill. Under Chapter 188 of the City Code, Santiago said, bulk waste is billed at $25 per two cubic yards plus set item fees, which puts the August 25 pickup (one pile, a chair, and two TVs) at $50. The invoice added six "claw grabs" at $25 each, a charge Santiago said appears nowhere in the code. A written dispute with photos was denied without explanation, Santiago said, and two requests to the mayor's office went unanswered. Santiago thanked Steele and Struber for trying to help, and asked the council to have the treasurer correct the invoice before it lands on the property tax bill and to audit the use of claw-grab fees.

Frank Maben of North Central Troy raised four issues: a crossing guard near the school between Ingalls and Douw, where Maben said children jump out of cars and run across the street; repairs at the 10th Street park, which Maben said has been overlooked for 25 to 30 years and isn't on the city's list of parks; action on a vacant, animal-infested building across from 2600 Sixth Avenue, the former John's Home Supply; and a police substation near Swift and Glen after more shootings there. Maben thanked the Planning Committee and Danae Zeigler for the new paving on Ingalls, the last phase of a federal Housing and Urban Development (HUD) grant.

Simona Fish, community engagement coordinator for Capital District Tobacco-Free Communities, asked the council to add vaping to Troy's smoke-free park rules. The "no smoking, no vaping" signs that went up in parks last year have no ordinance behind them, Fish said, after checking with City Hall. Albany, North Greenbush, and Cohoes updated their laws last year, and the group offers free signs and technical help. Ashita, a member of the youth group Reality Check, shared results of a Troy Farmers Market poll on who should be responsible for disposable vape waste, which contains lithium-ion batteries and heavy metals. Most votes went to consumers and manufacturers.
