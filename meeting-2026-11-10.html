Planning Budget & Capital Plan

November 10, 2026 | Troy City Council

← Back ▶ Watch on YouTube Budget Hearing Schedule Proposed 2027 Budget

No separate agenda or clerk's minutes for this session were posted in the city's Agenda Center as of November 23, 2026. Proceedings below are from the meeting recording. The session took no votes other than motions to adjourn. Some council questions weren't tied to a speaker on the recording and are attributed to "a council member."

Synopsis

The council's Planning Committee, chaired by Council Member Phil DiLorenzo, found a planning department running without a commissioner for about a year, with neither that job nor a vacant planner job funded for 2027. DiLorenzo said the department looks like it's running at two-thirds capacity. "Some of us feel you are stretched and would like to see those positions filled," Council President Sue Steele told the staff.

In the capital plan review that followed, the administration said the Riverwalk extension is about $3 million short and a bond request is coming. Council Member Shikole Struber pressed on why the plan prices a golf course sprayer two years out but has no money or estimate for safe walking access to Frear Park from North Central. "Not everybody can afford a car," she said.

A Planning Department Without a Commissioner

Planning Committee, chaired by Council Member DiLorenzo. Council President Steele opened with the Pledge of Allegiance. Called to order at 6:03 p.m. Present: Council Members DiLorenzo, Struber, Noreen McKee, Kathleen Spain-McLaren, and Greg Campbell-Cohen, and Council President Steele. Council Member Jona Favreau was excused. In attendance: Mayor Carmella Mantello, Deputy Mayor Seamus Donnelly, Comptroller Michael McNeff, the city's economic development director, and Carolyn, the Community Development Block Grant (CDBG) supervisor. The Planning hearing adjourned on a motion by Spain-McLaren, seconded by Struber, and the council moved straight into the capital plan.

The 2027 planning budget drops from $1.1 million to $532,000, a 54 percent cut on paper. Much of that is bookkeeping. A $382,400 state Department of Environmental Conservation (DEC) forestry grant for Prospect Park was booked in the 2026 planning budget and won't repeat unless the city wins it again. McNeff said the city is closing out the separate fund that held federal American Rescue Plan Act (ARPA) money and other grants, so grants now show up in department budgets.

The rest of the drop is people. The commissioner of planning job has been empty for about a year, and one of two planner jobs since a retirement in March. Neither is funded for 2027. Hiring is paused in every department, the administration said, but it will consider filling either job case by case.

DiLorenzo asked whether staff are stretched too thin. Donnelly said no: projects are now run jointly by planning, engineering, and General Services, and the city has a full-time engineer, Chris Marini, after years with a part-time one or none. Spain-McLaren asked whether the $98,000 commissioner salary is competitive. Donnelly said the city has interviewed a few people without finding the right fit and would raise the pay for the right candidate. Carolyn, the CDBG supervisor, now supervises the planning office as well.

The administration also hired Andy Burick, a land-use attorney, as a part-time deputy corporation counsel to advise the planning and zoning boards. Spain-McLaren, who served on the zoning board, called him "amazing to work with." Proposals for new planning software, which would let residents track projects and their environmental review online, are in, with a vendor expected in the coming weeks. Its cost isn't in the planning budget, and staff couldn't say what it is.

Riverwalk Extension Short $3 Million

Steele asked who is driving projects like the Riverwalk without a planning commissioner. The city's grant writer, the administration said. The extension would carry the path north from City Hall to about Jay Street. It has $4 million in state grants and a gap of about $3 million. After other grant applications fell through, Donnelly said, the administration will bring the council a bond resolution to close it, in part so the city spends its Downtown Revitalization Initiative (DRI) money on time.

The Prospect Park master plan is waiting until spring 2027, the mayor said, so residents aren't asked for input months before work can start. Wayfinding signs also need more money, though a first phase is coming.

Planning Line Items

Training drops $1,000 to $1,500, which DiLorenzo said disappointed him for a small staff. The administration said last year's training money went partly unspent and staff will favor training sessions over conferences. Spain-McLaren noted that only $500 is budgeted to train planning and zoning board members. Staff said board members mostly attend free local sessions, and Burick may run an in-house training.

Dues and subscriptions drop $5,000, partly from shortening required legal notices. Consultant services drop about $10,000; that line pays for the Building Blocks software several departments use and the city's share of CDTA bike share. The CDBG office is fully staffed, and a $35,000 part-time salary ends with the retirement of an employee after 60 years with the city.

DiLorenzo also noted the council is still waiting, almost three weeks later, for the Information Services dues and subscriptions breakdown it requested after seeing a very large increase in that line. Donnelly said it is on its way.

Capital Plan: Frear Park and North Central Access

Capital plan review, Council President Steele presiding. Donnelly reminded the council that the capital plan is a planning document. Nothing in it is funded until the administration brings a bond or funding request to the council.

Struber asked whether any of the planned pedestrian work at Frear Park helps people get there from North Central. No, the mayor said. A sidewalk up from North Central isn't funded and hasn't been priced, and could cost $1 million or more. "Not everybody can afford a car," Struber said, and many North Central residents walk or bike to the park. Crossing at Oakwood Avenue "is terrifying," she said. She was disappointed the plan could price a golf course sprayer two years out but not North Central access. The mayor called that "apples to oranges." A council member suggested a cheaper option: sidewalks from Oakwood to 15th Street and a trail up the hill.

The plan shows $2 million for the North Lake playground. The city lost the NY Plays grant that would have built a fully accessible playground, and the administration now plans a scaled-back version, combined with tennis courts on the northeast side of the lake, while it keeps applying for grants. Spain-McLaren asked where the tennis courts went, since they were in the original Frear Park plan. The mayor said they're part of that next phase. A walkway along North Lake Avenue, where the old parking lot was, should go in within weeks.

Donnelly estimated the Frear Park pavilion, which will house a restaurant and community space, at $7 million: about $3 million already borrowed, a little over $2 million in ARPA money, and more bonding under the $6 million the council authorized. The city's restaurant announcement described the restaurant as part of a broader $6.5 million Frear Park investment that also covers the playgrounds, trails, courts, and parking. Donnelly said the city doesn't expect to use the full bond authorization. The playground near the ice rink will be substantially done by the end of 2026.

A council member said the parking lot past the old Park Pub is pitch dark and a child reportedly fell there the night before. The parks commissioner is working on lighting, temporary if necessary, the administration said. Another council member asked for attention to the 10th Street pocket park, where friends of a young man who recently died gathered night after night. The administration said that court was resurfaced and the park is on its list. The Sheridan Avenue park at Thomas Street gets a resurfaced basketball court in spring.

Downtown: Congress and Ferry, Parking, and One Monument Square

The plan lists $5.8 million for Congress and Ferry streets in 2027, but Donnelly said the full corridor project is closer to $13.5 million in construction, mostly state-funded, with $8 million to $9 million more in 2028. Bids go out in spring 2027, most work happens in 2028, and the local match could be about 5 percent. A council member asked about the second traffic lane at the top of Congress Street, calling it "a lane for bad behavior." Donnelly said calming that intersection is being looked at in the final design.

About $433,000 in DRI money, plus Troy Industrial Development Agency (IDA) funds, starts a phased wayfinding signage project. The plan also includes a study of parking software and garages: permit sales are capped at the number of spaces, meters break down often, and the elevator in the garage behind the new City Hall is being repaired.

The One Monument Square redevelopment and a new phase of Chamberlain Riverfront Park are one project run by the developer. A council member asked about the $2 million in ARPA money. It goes to the Troy Local Development Corporation (LDC) before year end for preliminary design, which satisfies federal spending deadlines, and the developer expects to spend it by then. The city's One Monument Square announcement lists the same $2 million ARPA commitment toward a roughly $50 million project.

Vehicles, the DPW Complex, and Public Safety Buildings

The plan staggers purchases of plow-equipped dump trucks, mini dump trucks for narrow streets like Lansingburgh's boulevards, City Hall pool cars, a heavy-duty lift for the central garage, and a street sweeper to replace one in disrepair. Donnelly said the Department of Public Works (DPW) complex needs fencing, lighting, enclosed salt sheds, garage work, and a new fuel pump island, which is tied to DEC notices of violation.

Steele asked who gets city vehicles. Donnelly and the mayor each have one, as do the city engineer, the General Services commissioner, and on-call supervisors in police, fire, and Public Utilities. Code officers, planning staff, and IT share the pool cars. Mechanics have asked the city to stop buying electric vehicles, Donnelly said, because the ones it bought needed costly repairs. The $100,000 pool car line would buy two or three small SUVs from savings or fund balance. "We would not want to bond for vehicles like this," Donnelly said.

A fully equipped police SUV runs about $85,000, so the $250,000 patrol line covers two or three. Police headquarters needs masonry repairs, likely just under $200,000 to start, and the firehouses need masonry and apron work; those would be bonded. The fire rescue truck the council approved this year may not arrive until late 2028 or 2029. Police and fire operating budgets were reviewed at the November 9 hearing.

Streets, Lights, and Water

Street lights out citywide have dropped from more than 600 to about 100, the administration said. The tall poles cost $15,000 or more to replace, and the LED heads installed all at once in 2022 will start failing around the same time. Campbell-Cohen asked whether the promised $1 million in savings from that conversion has materialized. Donnelly will check. The mayor, who voted for the conversion as a council member, said the city isn't seeing it, because it now pays outside contractors to service lights National Grid used to maintain.

A council member asked how much will go to the city's ADA transition plan, its schedule for making streets and sidewalks accessible under the Americans with Disabilities Act. Nothing specific, the administration said, though ADA ramps and curbs are added wherever crews work. A consultant has spent the year building the city's first risk management and employee safety program. The annual infrastructure program pairs paving with water main work, as on Fifth Avenue and Hill Street in Little Italy.

Public Utilities items include phase two of the water transmission main, delayed by a redesign and private property easements; an assessment of the city's dams; removing manganese sludge from the water plant lagoons, which overflow into a creek in heavy rain and have drawn DEC violations; new water plant controls; and a permanent, grant-funded corrosion control feeder that keeps lead from leaching into the water.

The city has replaced more than 900 lead service lines since 2024, the mayor said, and crews will work until the ground freezes. The city's April 2026 announcement put the count at more than 400, and $16.7 million in state and federal funding announced in December 2025 covers about 1,100 more. A council member asked whether homes with young children still get priority. Yes, Donnelly said. The member pointed to a stretch of Fourth Street where nearly every home has a young child and lead or unknown pipes, and to Stow Hill, where Donnelly said lead work starts this year ahead of a full street rebuild next year.

No garbage trucks are in the plan because the council already approved them this year. The plan does include new public trash and recycling bins with lids.
