Public Works, Parks & Sanitation Budget Hearing

November 12, 2026 | Troy City Council

← Back ▶ Watch on YouTube Budget Hearing Schedule Proposed 2027 Budget

No separate agenda or clerk's minutes for this session were posted in the city's Agenda Center as of November 23, 2026. Proceedings below are from the meeting recording. The session took no votes other than the motion to adjourn. Many council questions weren't tied to a speaker on the recording and are attributed to "a council member."

Synopsis

The council's General Services Committee, chaired by Council Member Jona Favreau, spent more than two hours on the 2027 budgets for code enforcement, engineering, facilities, streets, parks, and sanitation. Favreau questioned a $74,675 deputy director job that was filled without a line in the 2026 budget, and a council member backed a higher salary for a deputy city engineer as a check on what contractors charge the city.

Council Member Noreen McKee asked who pays the insurance on the new City Hall, which the administration had promised would save $100,000. Deputy Mayor Seamus Donnelly said the Troy Local Development Corporation (LDC) covers it and couldn't give the cost. The administration also said it is going through six or seven long-standing Siemens service agreements one by one because the city didn't understand what it was paying for.

A Job Filled Without a Budget Line

General Services Committee, chaired by Council Member Favreau. Council President Sue Steele opened with the Pledge of Allegiance. Called to order at 6:02 p.m. Present: Council Members Favreau, Phil DiLorenzo, Greg Campbell-Cohen, Shikole Struber, Kathleen Spain-McLaren, and McKee, and Council President Steele. In attendance: Mayor Carmella Mantello, Deputy Mayor Donnelly, General Services Commissioner Joseph Mazzariello, Comptroller Michael McNeff, a staff member from the treasurer's office, and the heads of each division. A citywide Microsoft Outlook outage meant year-to-date figures were handed out on paper. The hearing ran well past two hours, with a short break, and ended with public comment.

Favreau asked about a deputy director of General Services job, at $74,675, that wasn't in the 2026 budget but was filled anyway and paid from engineering's unspent salary money. She pointed out that the council was asked to approve the deputy mayor's private secretary nine months after that job was filled. Donnelly said civil service creates jobs, not the council, and that the administration only needs council approval for a transfer when salary savings run out. A council member asked whether that means every vacant job listed as salary savings could be filled without coming to the council. Yes, Donnelly said, unless the savings run out.

City Engineer Chris Marini said the higher consultant line covers urgent work like the site management plan at the former Scolite site. Struber asked why the empty deputy city engineer job rose from $90,000 to $105,000. Marini said that's what it will take to hire someone. A council member backed the job as a likely money saver: "A lot of contractors and consultants, they can find $105,000 very easily in a project if there isn't somebody scrutinizing it." The member asked the administration not to shift the money to another job if it stays unfilled. "It's a tight budget. I'm sticking my neck out for you here."

The New City Hall and the Siemens Agreements

McKee asked where insurance for the new City Hall, in the former Proctor's building, shows up in the budget. Donnelly said the LDC, which owns the building, pays the property insurance, while the city keeps its liability policy. He couldn't give that cost. McKee pressed: under a triple net lease, where the tenant normally covers insurance, taxes, and upkeep, the city should be paying it, and the administration had promised $100,000 in savings. Donnelly said this isn't a traditional triple net lease and that the LDC board chose to absorb the insurance. "I know you really want that charge to be relayed to the taxpayer," Donnelly said, and asked McKee whether taxpayers should pay it if the LDC doesn't.

Rent rises from $535,000 to about $669,400, which Donnelly said is the city's whole annual payment for the new building. The budget adds $100,000 in temporary salaries for security at City Hall during business hours and meetings, likely retired police officers who would also greet visitors, freeing the officer now posted there to go back on patrol. Struber asked why utilities aren't lower given the city's National Grid credits. Facilities head Chris Kavanaugh said the credits only apply to electric; the new building is largely electric, but staff budgeted the higher amount until real bills come in.

Most of the facilities consultant line pays six or seven long-standing Siemens service and energy-savings agreements that predate this administration, Mazzariello said. Staff are going through them one by one because the city didn't understand what it was paying for. Donnelly gave an example: one agreement billed the city for energy savings on an HVAC system in a parking garage that has no HVAC. A council member asked whether anyone had looked at an industrial heat pump for the new City Hall. No one had.

Code Enforcement and the Vacant Building Registry

A council member asked whether the administration still won't let council members see the vacant building registry. Donnelly said the city keeps it private for safety reasons, and agreed to ask Acting Corporation Counsel Richard Morrissey about letting council members see it under a confidentiality agreement.

Favreau noted vacant building fees were at $463,000 through the third quarter against a $950,000 estimate. Staff said most of those fees come in around November, before unpaid ones go on tax bills. She also found the landlord registry line zeroed out. Staff said those costs are mixed into postage and printing, so the budget can't show what code spends on its own notices versus the county's program. Donnelly said the city's new Tyler Technologies financial software should fix errors like this.

Four code jobs are open: two rental registry inspectors paid by the county's new lead paint program, an assistant code inspector, and a plans examiner. Asked what a rental inspection program would take, staff said a 2014 study estimated four to five more employees. Donnelly said the county lead program, which inspects older rental homes and keeps a registry, works as a partial test of one; that state money runs three years as a pilot. Struber asked whether the city can use the lead program's data for its own registry.

Mayor Mantello opened the hearing by saying Red X buildings, the city's warning to firefighters that a building is unsafe to enter, are down from more than 80 to 25, and vacant buildings from nearly 500 in 2024 to 310, with 115 of those under active permits. Those counts don't appear in the city's press releases, and the council hasn't been allowed to see the registry they come from.

Traffic Signals From 1934 and a Short-Staffed Crew

Traffic control supervisor Bill Wangler is down to three people after two retirements, and possibly two after a coming surgery. The two open jobs are deferred to the end of 2027. The crew maintains about 2,500 signs and most traffic signals, and has started on the city's roughly 4,200 street lights. Favreau said the crossing signals on Pawling Avenue haven't worked for years. Thirty intersections, mostly in South Troy and Lansingburgh, still run mechanical signals designed in 1934. Parts are nearly impossible to find, and Wangler puts light bulbs in the cabinets to keep them warm enough to work. Replacing one can cost $50,000.

Streets lose two deferred jobs and $75,000 in temporary salaries after a "hotspot crew" tried last year didn't work out. Street light utilities drop from $300,000 to $200,000 because National Grid credits now cover those bills. A council member asked why keep $200,000; Mazzariello called it a contingency. Salt barns are full.

The central garage's vehicle parts budget drops to $230,000, even though the council added money this year through Ordinance 47. Mazzariello said three new garbage trucks will cut repair bills, and asked the council to consider a lease-to-own street sweeper.

Parking garage revenue is on pace for about $420,000, roughly $80,000 under budget. Donnelly said he has tried and failed to buy a garage permit online; the city is choosing among three vendors to put meters, permits, and payments in one system. A council member noted the city code may only exempt Sundays from meter fees, while the meters and signs say weekends.

Parks, the Marina, Golf, and Youth

The parks budget shows 13 positions but only six on staff, with five laborers deferred to the end of 2027. Parks supervisor Kevin Graber said the crew has managed that way for years. Knickerbacker ice rink fees reached $188,000, ahead of budget. Favreau flagged a rink health insurance line jumping from $28,000 to $69,000 for two employees; staff said it will be sorted out when the bills are charged.

Marina fuel sales reached $147,000 and docking $41,000 through the third quarter, about $60,000 short. Staff expect about 18 American Cruise Lines visits at $3,500 each, not yet billed, to close the gap. DiLorenzo asked why installing and removing the docks is so expensive. It takes a crane on the water and few companies do it, staff said; the contract goes out to bid after one more season.

Frear Park golf brought in more than $1.3 million and hosted more than 40 charity tournaments, staff said. Golf pro Josh Bule said resident play set a record and the course still doesn't have enough tee times. Bule plans to raise non-resident rates and start a junior program with both Boys and Girls Clubs. A council member asked whether the city could take a share of restaurant sales; Donnelly said the contract for the future Frear Park pavilion includes that.

The Youth Bureau's youth specialist job is gone. The mayor said community outreach director Kevin Prior and nonprofit partners do that work. The contract line rises $150,000, mostly to pay the Boys and Girls Clubs to staff a second city pool. The two senior centers get $5,000 each, and the East Side center, which missed the 60 percent residency rule this year and got a waiver, has been told to raise its Troy membership.

Sanitation and the Garbage Fee

The garbage fee stays at this year's level after a $25 cut last year, the mayor said. The proposed 2027 budget holds it at $229 per unit, and the garbage fund still needs about $1.13 million from the General Fund to break even. Sanitation spending rises about 7 percent, or $400,000.

Through the third quarter, violations brought in $503,000, bulk pickup $55,000, and commercial pickup $90,000. Favreau found lines for sales tax and gifts and donations in the garbage fund that no one could explain; McNeff will look into them. Charges the garbage fund pays the General Fund for services jump from $577,000 to $868,000, and a council member asked for the math behind that number. The household hazardous waste line, about $100,000, is partly paid back by the county.

Public Comment

Michelle Carney of District 4 said so many street lights are out or dim that walking at night is dangerous, and that they have tripped and fallen. Carney asked who is responsible for repairs, how much the city has spent, and whether Siemens still has a role. A council member pointed to the city website, where residents can report a light by the number on its pole.

Arthur Wang of 14th Street, District 3, questioned the roughly $700,000 Frear Park tent, which Wang described as a "glass matchbox" that has to come down every winter. The administration said the $700,000 was mostly the price of the tent itself, bought used from a club in Saratoga, and a local company removes and stores the roof each winter because it can't hold snow.

A District 5 resident urged the city to stop contracting with Siemens, citing the company's use of forced labor during World War II and its work on an electricity link to Israel, and asked whether city purchasing rules could screen contractors on ethics and favor local businesses and workers. A council member said the city has no current Siemens projects, only older agreements, and invited the resident to the open public forum at the end of every regular and finance meeting.
